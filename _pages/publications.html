Job Market Paper

  • Diagnosed Too Soon or Too Late? Labor Market and Health Outcomes of Adults with Early vs. Late ADHD Diagnosis [ SSRN Working Paper No. 5452997 ].

    The study examines whether the timing of ADHD diagnosis matters. Using nationally representative U.S. Medical Expenditure Panel Survey data from 2008–2022 and a within-diagnosed design, I estimate average treatment effects with augmented inverse probability weighting and corroborate the findings with propensity score matching, instrumental-variable estimates, and double machine learning. Earlier diagnosis delivers durable long-term health gains, raises work hours by 13.3%, and increases years of schooling; yet average labor income lags relative to later-diagnosed counterparts, with the wage penalty larger among those with more severe symptoms. A mechanism-consistent account emerges from wage equations and a Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition: Mincer marginal returns are lower for the early-diagnosed (schooling about 4.6% versus 9.4%; experience about 3.3% versus 9.2%), and the pricing of the same endowments is adverse for those diagnosed early. Timing matters: effects are strongest at ages 5–6 (work hours rise to 15.7%; health improvements largest) and remain stable through age 12. The findings motivate pairing very early screening and integrated healthcare interventions with labor-market tools that raise realized wage returns and mitigate structural barriers. Policy implications are discussed.

Publications

  • Stability analyses on the effect of vaccination and contact tracing in monkeypox virus transmission
    [ J. Math. Comput. Sci. 13 (2023), Article ID 8 ].

    This study builds a monkeypox transmission model that integrates vaccination, quarantine, and contact tracing, and its sensitivity findings give public health policy clear targets: higher vaccination coverage together with effective tracing stabilizes the disease-free state, sharply lowers outbreak risk, and protects community well-being (Joint with Solomon Eshun and James Ladzekpo).

Working Papers

  • In Whom We Trust? Social Networks and Trust in AI vs. Human Experts in Housing Investment Decisions
    [ Revise and Resubmit at Finance Research Letters. SSRN Working Paper No. 5402137 ].

    Using propensity score matching and inverse probability weighting on nationally representative data from an SSRS randomized experiment with the FINRA Investor Education Foundation, this paper estimates how offline and online social networks shape consumer trust in AI-generated versus human expert housing advice. Offline networks increase trust in human experts, especially among financially literate midlife adults. Online networks are associated with greater trust in AI, particularly among younger adults, political liberals, and mobile home residents. The results highlight the role of social context for policy and platform design in digital finance (Joint with Prince Bosompem and Jodi Letkiewicz).

  • Buy Now, Struggle Later? Generational Differences in BNPL Use and Household Financial Behaviors
    [ Revise and Resubmit at International Journal of Bank Marketing (IJBM) ].

    Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL) promises instant gratification, but does it quietly erode financial resilience across generations? Drawing on U.S. household data, this study uncovers that BNPL use is strongly tied to unhealthy short-term financial behaviors like mounting credit-card debt and weaker emergency savings, particularly among older cohorts. Yet, its influence on long-term planning, such as investment and retirement, appears negligible. By revealing how hyperbolic discounting shapes financial choices differently for Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, and Gen Z, this paper highlights an urgent need for tailored financial literacy programs and regulatory oversight to safeguard households in an era of frictionless credit (Joint with Prince Bosompim).

  • Who Benefits from Residential Solar? Evidence on Energy Insecurity Disparities and Policy Gaps in the U.S.
    [ Under review at Energy Policy. SSRN Working Paper No. 5503835 ].

    This study investigates the impact of residential solar photovoltaic (PV) adoption on energy insecurity outcomes in the United States, with particular attention to disparities across generational cohorts, racial groups, and income levels. Inverse probability weighting is applied to nationally representative data from the 2020 Residential Energy Consumption Survey to estimate average treatment effects across three dimensions of energy insecurity: prolonged power outages, disconnection notices, and affordability-related tradeoffs. Results indicate that solar PV adoption reduces the likelihood of outages and disconnections, although its effect on affordability remains limited. Subgroup analysis reveals that older cohorts, particularly Baby Boomers and Generation X, consistently benefit, while Millennials and Generation Z face uneven outcomes. Racial and income-based gaps persist, with White and middle-income households gaining more reliable benefits. These findings highlight the need for targeted policy solutions, including bundled assistance for low-income households and generation-specific program design. Without such structural reforms, solar adoption risks reinforcing existing inequities rather than resolving them. Policy implications are discussed.

  • When Assistance Isn't Enough: Energy Insecurity Among Older Adults and Policy Considerations for Energy Assistance Programs [ Under Review — Applied Economics. SSRN Working Paper No. 5208234 ].

    Using inverse probability weighting to estimate the average treatment effect on the 2020 Residential Energy Consumption Survey, this paper finds that bill-assistance recipients remain more likely to face outages, disconnection notices, and affordability tradeoffs, evidence that aid often reaches households already in crisis rather than preventing insecurity. Effects are strongest for homeowners in family houses, weaker for renters and mobile-home residents, and modestly buffered by shade trees. Policy recommendations include shifting from one-time grants to monthly benefits aligned with billing cycles, increasing grant values, expanding outreach to underserved housing types, integrating delivery with utility communication systems, and investing in green infrastructure.

  • Labor Market Margins and the Affordable Care Act's Health Insurance Reforms: Racial Heterogeneity Among Pre-Retirees [ SSRN Working Paper No. 5453054 ].

    If government wants more older pre retirees to remain employed rather than retire early and strain Social Security, can ACA Marketplace premium subsidies and Medicaid do the job, and by how much? Using optimal propensity score matching with g computation and a staggered difference-in-differences design on American Community Survey data. The results show that subsidizing approximately 132 preretirees is associated with one additional preretiree remaining attached to the labor force, and subsidizing approximately 66 preretirees is associated with one additional preretiree shifting to part time employment without exit while Medicaid expansion shows no aggregate labor-market effect. Distributional patterns are clear: gains are larger for White and Asian workers and weaker for Black and Hispanic workers, pointing to coverage paired with targeted labor-market supports.

  • Mortality Risk Under Occupational and Health Comorbidity: Double Machine Learning Assessment of the Obesity–Diabetes Double Burden [ SSRN Working Paper No. 5453014 ].

    This paper quantifies how mortality risk escalates when occupational exposure intersects with the obesity–diabetes double burden. Using double machine learning with orthogonalized estimation, I recover robust effects under high-dimensional confounding and map heterogeneity across occupations and worker risk profiles. The evidence indicates that the double burden raises mortality risk beyond additive effects, with the largest excess risk concentrated in more hazardous or physically demanding occupations, public-sector jobs, and among those with a history of hypertension and smoking. Policy implications point to coupling metabolic prevention and obesity/diabetes management with targeted workplace protections and benefits design.

  • Predicting Obesity Risk Through Gene-Level Modeling: A Machine Learning Approach to Inform Precision Public Health Policy [ Slides: ORI 8th Annual Meeting (Slide #11), 2023 ].

    Can gene-level signals help us get ahead of the obesity epidemic? Leveraging DNA methylation and HPA-axis–focused feature discovery (SAM + RFE-SVM), this study builds a multilayer perceptron machine-learning model that predicts individual obesity risk with strong out-of-sample performance (≈83% accuracy, 90% recall, 75% AUC). The results show how gene-based risk stratification can sharpen prevention, target resources, and inform cost-effective, precision public health policy (Joint with Rodrigo Hansapani and Vincent Diego).

Books

  • Machine Learning Feature Selection Techniques to Model the Elements of Cash Conversion Cycle and Other Covariates on Hospital Performance
    [ B.P. International, 2024. DOI: 10.9734/bpi/rumcs/v8/440 ].

    This book models hospitals’ financial health using machine learning in Python (scikit-learn), testing exhaustive best-subset search alongside Lasso, Ridge, and Elastic Net on real hospital data. Lasso yields a compact, high-signal feature set with RMSE comparable to peers, consistently elevating Accounts Receivable Days, Accounts Payable Days, Inventory Turnover Days, and Debt Ratio as key drivers. Each one-day acceleration in collections aligns with ≈1% performance improvement, while each additional day in payables corresponds to a ~0.003 decline, offering ML-driven guidance for leaders focused on strengthening hospital health through cash-flow discipline.
