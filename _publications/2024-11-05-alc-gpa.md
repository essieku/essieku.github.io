### Abstract
I examine whether the timing of ADHD diagnosis matters. Using nationally representative Medical Expenditure Panel Survey data and a within diagnosed design, I estimate average treatment effects using augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW) estimator and corroborate the findings with propensity score matching, instrumental-variable estimates, and double machine learning. Earlier diagnosis delivers durable long-term health gains, raises work hours by 13.3% and increases years of schooling, yet average labor income lags relative to later-diagnosed counterparts. A mechanism consistent account emerges from wage equations and a Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition: Mincer marginal returns are lower for the early diagnosed (schooling about 4.6% versus 9.4%; experience about 3.3% versus 9.2%) and the pricing of the same endowments is adverse. Timing matters: effects are strongest at ages 5-6 (work hours raise to 15.7%; health improvements largest) and remain stable through age 12. The findings motivate pairing very early screening and integrating healthcare interventions with labor market tools that raise realized wage returns and mitigate structural barriers. Policy implications are discussed.
